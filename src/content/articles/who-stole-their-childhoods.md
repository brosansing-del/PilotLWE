A child wakes up and looks for her mother.

She isn’t there. Perhaps she is living with grandparents. Perhaps she is at a residential school. Perhaps she has not seen her father for months. The adults around her may know where her parents are, but they cannot necessarily tell her. There is no ordinary family routine, no parent waiting at the school gate, and no certainty about when the absent parent will return.

For a child growing up in a Maoist family, this can become normal life. Underground warfare demands secrecy, mobility and complete commitment from its cadres. When cadres become parents, their families must adapt to those demands. Former cadres have described children being left with grandparents or relatives and, in some cases, being sent to residential schools while their parents remained underground. For an adult who joins an insurgency, separation from family may be understood as a political sacrifice. A child has no such choice. The child simply grows up within the consequences of that decision.

> “I was 12 when I heard my ma was killed in an encounter. I never saw her. And I never understood why she left.” — Badru

The problem can begin even before birth. Accounts from surrendered cadres, documented by the Indian government and reported by the press, describe male cadres being subjected to vasectomy and women facing pressure to terminate pregnancies. Some former cadres later underwent sterilization-reversal procedures after leaving the movement because they wanted children.

These measures illustrate how far organizational control could extend into private life. Reproduction was not simply a personal matter when parenthood was considered incompatible with underground warfare. For children who were born, the consequences were different but equally personal. A guerrilla cannot easily maintain a stable household or provide continuous childcare. A child may know that a parent is alive without actually experiencing that parent as part of everyday life.

The emotional consequences are difficult to separate from the practical ones. A child may grow up with grandparents or other caregivers, attend school away from home and encounter parents only occasionally. Questions that other children take for granted— Where is my mother? When will my father come home? Why can’t we live together?— can acquire a political dimension.

The irony is striking. Maoism presents itself as a struggle to create a better future for oppressed communities and their children. Yet the underground structure required to pursue that struggle can deprive some of its own children of the stability that childhood requires. Some former cadres eventually surrendered because they wanted an ordinary family life: to marry, live with their partners and raise children. Leaving the movement could therefore represent not merely a political change, but a return to family life.

None of this requires idealising conventional families. Families can themselves reproduce patriarchy, violence and inequality. Nor should every allegation made by former cadres be accepted without considering its evidentiary basis. But those qualifications do not erase the central issue.

> “My dad died trying to save his commander. I had already lost my mom and grew up an orphan. Now those cadres have surrendered. What did the revolution ever gave me? All it did was take away everything I had.” — Raghu from Bastar

The children did not choose the underground.

They did not choose secrecy, separation or the political commitments of their parents. Yet they could be required to live with all three. This is what makes the experience of children an important lens through which to examine Indian

Maoism. The movement’s adult members could understand their sacrifices as part of a political struggle. For their children, those sacrifices could simply mean an absent parent and an unusual childhood.

A revolution may demand sacrifice from those who join it. But when that sacrifice is inherited by children who never consented to it, the question becomes more difficult: who is paying the price for the revolution?

For the cadre, separation may be a political choice.

For the child, it is simply childhood.
