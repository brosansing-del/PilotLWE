> ‘I was 29 when I underwent abortion. I was starting to have second thoughts about staying in the organization.. I wanted to keep the child, but that was not my decision to make.. now I am told that I will never be able to conceive..’ — Sameela, Narayanpur

One of the less discussed aspects of Indian Maoism is its treatment of marriage, reproduction and family life. The movement speaks of liberation from oppressive social structures, including patriarchy. Yet within the underground organization, even the most intimate decisions—whom a cadre could marry, whether they could have children and when they could live with their spouse —could become matters of organizational control.

Marriage was permitted in some circumstances, but family life was treated as a potential threat to revolutionary commitment. Accounts from surrendered cadres describe couples being permitted to marry while being prevented from having children because parenthood was considered incompatible with the mobility and discipline required for armed struggle. Multiple channels reported allegations from surrendered cadres that male cadres were forced to undergo vasectomy before marriage and that women who became pregnant could be compelled to undergo abortions.

The most striking evidence comes from former cadres themselves. Several surrendered Maoists underwent vasectomy reversal after leaving the organization because they wanted to have children. Data from reliable sources indicate that around 140 surrendered cadres were successfully operated upon for reversal of sterilization. In one documented case, a former cadre who had been sterilized at a young age subsequently underwent reversal and became a father. This raises a question much larger than contraception.

Who owns the revolutionary cadre’s body—the individual or the organization?

A revolutionary movement may reasonably impose extraordinary restrictions during combat. But sterilization is not merely a military regulation. It permanently alters a person’s reproductive future. Deciding that a cadre may not become a parent because children might weaken revolutionary commitment transforms the organization from a political movement into an authority over the individual’s most private choices.

The consequences extend to children who never get to exist, as well as children who are separated from their parents. A child born into an underground insurgent family would necessarily impose obligations that conflict with guerrilla warfare. The organization therefore has an incentive to prevent ordinary family formation altogether. This produces a disturbing inversion: the revolutionary movement claims to build a society for future generations while requiring its own cadres to sacrifice the possibility of creating families in the present.

There is also a contradiction in the treatment of marriage itself. Accounts from former cadres describe marriages taking place within the organization, sometimes followed by restrictions on the couple’s ability to live together. Partners are given areas miles from each other to avoid emotional intimacy. Family relationships could be subordinated to organizational requirements, while emotional attachment itself was treated as something that could weaken revolutionary discipline.

This is particularly significant for women. Pregnancy, childbirth and childcare impose physical and emotional realities that cannot simply be subordinated to revolutionary schedules. If the organization regards motherhood as a military liability, women’s reproductive capacity becomes something to be managed for the needs of the movement.

Maoism promises liberation from structures that subordinate individuals to social power. Yet an organization that determines whether its members may marry, become parents or maintain family relationships reproduces another form of subordination. The contradiction is therefore deeply personal.

A cadre may be willing to risk death for the revolution. But the revolution also demands something quieter: the surrender of an ordinary human future—a spouse, a child, a home and the right to decide what happens to one’s own body.

That raises the ultimate question for any movement claiming to liberate human beings: If a revolution requires its own members to surrender control over love, reproduction and family, where exactly does liberation begin?
